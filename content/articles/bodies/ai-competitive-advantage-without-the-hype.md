My view: the competitive value of AI is in what a business can do better, not in how many AI tools it has bought. A quicker response, a more reliable handoff, or more capacity for useful work is worth investigating. An AI announcement is not the result.

That is also why I would not wait for the technology conversation to settle before looking at the work. You can take action without chasing every release or assuming your competitors have already solved it.

## Compete on the experience, not the label

Imagine two service businesses receiving similar customer inquiries. This is a hypothetical comparison, not a client case.

In one, the request sits until the right person gathers the details, works out who should handle it, and prepares a reply. In the other, the information is organized for review and the responsible person has a clear next action.

The second arrangement may create a better response process. But its value depends on accuracy, usefulness, and follow-through, not simply whether AI was involved.

The same question applies to your business: what would the customer or your team notice if the work improved?

I would rather start there than with a claim that another company is “AI-powered.”

## Investigating the opportunity is different from buying a tool

You do not need a subscription to every new product to take AI seriously. You need a way to identify where it could matter and decide what is worth testing.

For a business that repeatedly researches similar assignments, the opportunity could be reusable knowledge. For a company whose next steps depend on memory, it might be more consistent coordination. For a team with clear processes and suitable tools, the useful change could be better configuration rather than a new system.

Those are opportunities to investigate. The point is to make a business decision, not win a technology collection contest.

## Consider the cost of leaving the work unchanged

Pick a process that matters and describe what it asks of the team today. Where does work wait? What gets repeated? What becomes difficult when demand increases? What opportunity would be easier to pursue with a better process?

Now compare that with the effort of making a change, including implementation, review, and ongoing operation.

The cost of inaction need not be expressed as a dramatic revenue estimate. Sometimes the visible cost is that an owner spends evenings reconstructing information. Sometimes the business cannot confidently accept the next assignment. Sometimes a useful idea remains unexplored.

My recommendation is to make that cost explicit enough to discuss, rather than leave it hidden inside “that’s how we do it.”

## Protect your team’s attention

I do not think a company should have to turn every employee into an AI researcher before benefiting from the technology.

The people doing the work should help explain the process, review options, and learn to use the resulting system. Comparing tools, designing integrations, and maintaining a custom application are different responsibilities.

That is a reason to bring in appropriate specialist help, whether through an existing provider, an internal team, or a partner such as Fidelis. It is not a claim that every business needs the same kind of help.

The choice should protect attention as well as solve the technical problem.

## What would make an advantage last?

Access to a tool alone is not the part I would build a strategy around. I would focus on the way the business uses it: the information available, the quality of the process, the team's judgment, and the willingness to improve through use.

Ask whether the proposed system makes useful work reusable. Does it fit the team's responsibilities? Can mistakes be noticed and corrected? Can the approach change when the business changes?

These questions point toward a working capability rather than a demonstration that gets abandoned after the initial excitement.

## A next step that is neither panic nor delay

Write down one business goal and one process connected to it. Walk through a recent example with the person responsible. Identify the questions you cannot answer yet. Then investigate whether a process change, existing tool, integration, or custom system is worth pursuing.

You do not need to know the tools, or even where to start. What matters is creating a way to move from curiosity to an informed decision.
