Start with the research output your team needs, then compare whether Apollo, a custom workflow, or a combination can produce it. My recommendation is to evaluate fit, evidence quality, review effort, handoff, and ongoing cost, not assume that a custom build is automatically better.

A company can have access to a large contact database and still spend substantial effort producing a useful shortlist.

That does not automatically mean the database is the wrong purchase. It may mean the job includes more than finding contacts.

The decision I would investigate is not “Apollo or AI?” It is “What work does the business need completed, and which combination of tools can complete it well?”

That distinction is important because modern prospecting platforms already include more than static lists. Apollo’s documentation describes AI research and workflows that can update records, create tasks, or move contacts into sequences, subject to access and plan requirements.[^a3-1][^a3-2]

A fair comparison should start with what the available product actually does.

## What this comparison covers

This is a decision guide informed by vendor documentation, not a hands-on product benchmark or a ranking of providers. It does not compare current plan prices or promise a return on a custom build.

## Define the output before comparing features

“Find more leads” leaves too much unspecified.

Who should appear in the result? What would disqualify an account? What information does the team need before taking action? How fresh must it be? Where should the approved work go?

For a straightforward audience defined by common business characteristics, an existing platform may already meet the need with appropriate configuration.

For a more specialized research task, the team may need additional evidence, context from previous work, or a different review and handoff process.

Those are reasons to investigate customization. They are not proof that a vendor cannot help.

## Compare against representative examples

Choose a small set of real research assignments and examples of accounts the business would accept or reject.

Then compare the current process with the available alternatives. Can each find relevant results? Can the team understand why a result was included? How much checking is required? Does useful information get lost when the result moves into the next system?

Do not assess quality only by how many records appear. A large output can be expensive to review.

Equally, a short output is not automatically well targeted. Review a sample of rejected or missed accounts too, where practical, so the test is not limited to the system’s preferred answers.

This creates a more useful discussion than comparing the number of features on two sales pages.

## Three reasonable paths

**Use the platform more effectively.** The improvement may involve better search criteria, research fields, workflows, or operating habits rather than new software.

**Extend the platform.** Keep the parts that work and connect them to another source, a custom review step, or the team’s existing systems.

**Build a distinct research workflow.** This may make sense when the business requires a different information model, reusable research history, specialized criteria, or a user experience that existing tools do not address adequately.

A custom workflow can still use a commercial provider for data or enrichment. Building the workflow does not eliminate the cost of the underlying information.

Apollo, for example, documents credit consumption for activities including enrichment and AI research.[^a3-3] A comparison should account for actual use, not only the price of a seat.

## Be clear about what the research does not establish

A public signal is evidence to investigate, not proof that a company wants to buy. A job change, funding announcement, or relevant article can provide context without establishing purchase intent.

Similarly, a model-generated score is a prioritization aid unless it has been validated for a stronger interpretation. It should not be presented as a reliable probability of closing simply because it is expressed as a number.

I would design the review process around those limits. Preserve source links, make uncertainty visible, and give the team a way to correct the result.

## The full cost includes human work

Compare the time required to prepare, review, correct, and use the output. Include implementation, ongoing data and software costs, maintenance, and any integration work.

The relevant outcome might be a faster approved shortlist or less research repeated on the next assignment. It might be better coverage. It might be no meaningful improvement, which is also useful to learn before expanding the project.

Do not turn a faster research step into an automatic revenue promise. The business still needs a suitable offer, appropriate outreach, and the capacity to serve the customer.

## Choose the workflow, not a side

At Fidelis, research systems are one example of [custom systems and implementation](/what-we-build/), not a mandatory part of every engagement.

An existing platform, a custom application, or a combination can each be the right answer. Discovery should determine which is justified by the business need.

The best question is not whether custom software can replace a subscription. It is whether the proposed arrangement produces more useful work at a cost and level of effort the business can support.
